Dojo etiquette 礼儀

Karate begins and ends with the bow. Etiquette (reigi) is not a formality: it creates a frame of respect and safety where you can train hard without getting hurt. Traditions vary by association, sensei and country; here are the most common customs. When in doubt, ask your sensei or a senpai.

Entering and leaving the dojo

  • Bow when you enter the dojo and when you leave it, facing the front of the dojo (the shomen). In some dojos, you also bow just before stepping onto the mat.
  • Train barefoot: shoes stay at the entrance.
  • Arrive a little early. If you are late, wait at the edge of the mat, kneeling in seiza, until the sensei invites you to join the class.
  • Don’t leave the mat during class without the sensei’s permission.

The bow 礼

  • Ritsurei 立礼, the standing bow: from Musubi Dachi (heels together, feet open in a V), bend about 20 to 30° from the waist, back straight, hands along the thighs.
  • Facing a partner, keep your eyes on them as you bow: this is zanshin, awareness that never switches off.
  • Zarei 座礼, the kneeling bow: from seiza, place your hands on the floor in front of you (left, then right) and bow, then straighten up (right, then left).

Opening and closing the class

In many dojos, students line up by rank facing the sensei, then sit in seiza on command. Then come the calls:

  • Mokuso 黙想: meditation, eyes closed, to leave the day behind. Mokuso yame: end of meditation.
  • Shomen ni rei 正面に礼: bow to the front of the dojo.
  • Sensei ni rei 先生に礼: bow to the sensei.
  • Otagai ni rei お互いに礼: bow to each other.

The class ends the same way, often with the recitation of the Dojo Kun.

Kiai 気合

The kiai is the powerful shout that goes with a decisive technique. Ki (気) means “energy” and ai (合) “joining”: the shout joins energy and technique. It comes from the belly, not the throat, and empties the lungs at the moment of impact. In each kata, it falls at set moments, shown in red in the tutorials.

Osu 押忍

Osu (pronounced “oss”) is an all-purpose word among karateka: hello, yes, understood, thank you, keep going. It shows respect and the will to persevere. Its use varies a lot from one dojo to another: some say it after every instruction, others almost never. Do as your dojo does.

Addressing the sensei, and titles

  • In Japanese, the title follows the name: “Name-Sensei” (with the last name, sometimes the first name). In English- and French-speaking dojos, people often say “Sensei” alone, or “Sensei” followed by the name.
  • Senpai 先輩: a more advanced student who helps the younger ones (the kohai).
  • Shihan 師範: master instructor, a high-ranking black belt who also trains teachers.
  • Renshi 錬士, Kyoshi 教士, Hanshi 範士: teaching titles some associations award to senior ranks, from lowest to highest.

Dress and hygiene

  • A clean white dogi, and a well-tied belt (tying your belt).
  • Short nails, clean feet, long hair tied back, no jewellery: for yourself and for your partners.
  • To straighten your dogi or belt, turn toward the back of the dojo, away from the sensei and the shomen.

During class

  • Listen to instructions in silence and answer them (“Osu” or “Yes, Sensei”).
  • Don’t walk in front of the sensei or between them and the group; go around the back.
  • Control your techniques with a partner: hard training is never an excuse to hurt someone.
  • Bow to your partner before and after every partner drill.
