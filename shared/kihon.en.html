The basics (Kihon) 基本

These cards are a reminder of a white belt’s first techniques: how to stand, punch, block and kick. They don’t replace the sensei, who corrects what no page can see.

The curriculum

  • Kihon 基本, the basics: stances, punches, blocks and kicks, drilled in lines at every rank. The focus is on body movement, the target, stance accuracy and the application of each technique.
  • Kata 型, the forms: set sequences, performed the same way every time, to refine technique and make it effective. They are sometimes said to come from samurai swordsmanship (kenjutsu); historians doubt it, as Okinawan kata come mostly from Chinese martial arts. But the principle is the same: practising a pre-arranged fight alone, with no risk of hurting a partner. See The spirit of kata.
  • Kumite 組手, sparring with a partner: set drills first, then freer and freer (see below).
  • Bunkai 分解: analysing a kata, to understand what each movement does against an opponent.
  • Goshin Jutsu 護身術, self-defence: advanced techniques against wrist grabs, chokes, holds, and armed attacks.

You progress in this order: learn the movements, link them into kata, study their bunkai, then apply them with a partner to build confidence.

The fist (Seiken) 正拳

  1. Curl your fingers until each fingertip touches its base.
  2. Close them into the palm, then fold the thumb across the index and middle fingers.
  3. Strike with the first two knuckles, those of the index and middle fingers.
  4. Keep the wrist straight, in line with the forearm.
  5. Keep the fist relaxed on the way and tighten it only at the moment of impact (kime).

Stances (Tachikata) 立ち方

Musubi Dachi 結び立ち

Attention stance

  • Heels together, toes open in a V (about 45°).
  • Used for the bow (rei), at the start and at the end.

Shizentai 自然体 (Heiko Dachi, Hachiji Dachi)

Natural stance

  • Feet shoulder-width apart, parallel (Heiko Dachi) or with the toes slightly open (Hachiji Dachi).
  • Knees soft, barely bent.
  • The ready position (Yoi) and the stance for drills on the spot.

Zenkutsu Dachi 前屈立ち

Front stance

  • Feet a little wider than the shoulders sideways, and about two shoulder-widths long front to back.
  • The front foot points forward; the back foot is turned out about 30 to 45°.
  • The front knee is bent over the toes; the back leg is straight, with almost no bend.
  • The weight is about 60% in front and 40% behind.
  • The stance for stepping attacks and many blocks; the most common one in kata.

Kokutsu Dachi 後屈立ち and Kiba Dachi 騎馬立ち

Back stance and horse stance

  • Kokutsu Dachi: weight mostly on the back leg (about 70%), back knee well bent, feet at a right angle. Used for Shuto Uke in the Heian kata.
  • Kiba Dachi: feet parallel and wide, knees pushed outward, weight in the centre. The stance of the Tekki kata.

Punches (Tsuki) 突き

Choku Zuki 直突き

Straight punch, on the spot

  • From Shizentai. The non-punching hand is extended in front of you and marks the target (for example the solar plexus).
  • The fist leaves the hip palm up and turns at the end of its path to finish palm down, at impact.
  • At the same moment, the other hand pulls back to the hip (hikite): both arms work together.

Oi Zuki 追い突き

Lunge punch

  • A straight punch combined with a step forward into Zenkutsu Dachi.
  • Punch on the same side as the front leg; the hip and chest drive forward.
  • The fist lands at the same time as the foot.

Gyaku Zuki 逆突き

Reverse punch

  • From Zenkutsu Dachi, punch with the rear arm, on the side opposite the front leg.
  • The hips turn from sideways (hanmi) to facing forward (shomen): that is where the power comes from.
  • The front arm pulls back to the hip at the same time.

Blocks (Uke) 受け

Gedan Barai 下段払い

Downward sweep, against low attacks (kicks)

  • The blocking fist starts near the opposite ear; the other arm is extended downward, fist closed, at navel height.
  • The blocking arm slides down over the other one, which pulls back to the hip.
  • It finishes about two or three fists above the front knee, in Zenkutsu Dachi.
  • At first, pause at the preparation and exhale sharply as you block.

Age Uke 上げ受け

Rising block, against high or descending attacks

  • The non-blocking hand is extended in front of you and marks the target.
  • The blocking forearm rises, crossing in front of the face, while the other hand pulls back to the hip.
  • It finishes about one fist above the forehead, the forearm angled and turned outward, the body behind the block.

Kicks (Geri) 蹴り

Mae Geri Keage 前蹴り蹴上げ

Front snap kick

  • Strike with the ball of the foot (koshi), toes pulled well back.
  • Raise the knee first, then snap the leg out and pull it straight back, knee high, before setting the foot down.
  • The standing leg stays slightly bent and the stance stable; the upper body stays upright. At first, you can spread your arms a little for balance.
  • Usual targets: the knee, the groin, the solar plexus or the chin.

Kumite 組手, the progression

Partner sparring goes from the most structured to the freest. The exact order varies by school; here is the most common one in Shotokan:

  1. Gohon Kumite 五本組手: five announced attacks stepping forward, five blocks stepping back, one counterattack at the end.
  2. Sanbon Kumite 三本組手: the same in three steps.
  3. Kihon Ippon Kumite 基本一本組手: one announced attack, one block and one counterattack.
  4. Kaeshi Ippon Kumite 返し一本組手: one step with the roles switched: after defending, the defender attacks in turn.
  5. Jiyu Ippon Kumite 自由一本組手: one announced attack, but moving freely: distance and timing are not set.
  6. Jiyu Kumite 自由組手: free sparring.

Grading

Ranks go from kyu (級, coloured belts, counting down to 1st kyu) to dan (段, black belts, starting at 1st dan, shodan). Colours vary by association; each kata page shows the belt at which it is learned. See the full order in Belts and ranks (Obi).

  • To be eligible: you usually need a minimum training time since your last rank, good attendance and your sensei’s go-ahead.
  • The exam: kihon (combinations of techniques), the kata of the rank (one or more), and kumite, freer at higher ranks.
  • Mindset: prepare well, then ease off the stress: the exam measures your personal progress and earns you valuable advice for what comes next.
