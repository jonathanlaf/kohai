History of Shotokan 松濤館

Shotokan is the karate style that grew out of Funakoshi Gichin’s teaching. It is known for its low, strong stances, direct blocks, focused strikes and the place it gives to kata. It prefers a small set of powerful, well-aligned techniques to a multitude of variations.

You learn it the way you build a house: first a solid foundation (stances, basic techniques), then one storey at a time.

Okinawa, the cradle of karate 沖縄

Karate comes from Okinawa, an island south of mainland Japan, once the Ryūkyū Kingdom. Local fighting arts (te, 手, “the hand”) blended there with martial arts from China. For a long time they were taught privately, often in secret, to a few chosen students: learning from a true master was a great honour.

Funakoshi Gichin (1868–1957) 船越義珍

Funakoshi Gichin was born in 1868 in Shuri, Okinawa. A frail child, he began karate under two masters of the Shuri style, Azato Ankō and Itosu Ankō. He became a schoolteacher and, with Itosu, helped bring karate into Okinawa’s public schools.

To him, karate was not only a fighting art: it was a path of self-control and inner growth. He summed it up in the Dojo Kun and in his twenty principles, the Shōtō Nijū Kun 松濤二十訓.

1922: karate comes to Tokyo

In 1922, Funakoshi gave a karate demonstration in Tokyo, at an exhibition organized by the Ministry of Education. At the time, judo was far better known in Japan than karate. The demonstration drew so much interest that Kanō Jigorō, the founder of judo, invited him to the Kōdōkan. Funakoshi stayed in Japan and taught there, especially in university clubs.

In the 1930s, he changed the way the word karate is written: 唐手, “Chinese hand”, became 空手, “empty hand”. He added dō (道, “the way”): karate-dō is a way, not only a technique. He told his life story in Karate-Dō: My Way of Life.

The name “Shotokan”

Shōtō (松濤, “pine waves”, the sound of wind in the pines) was the pen name Funakoshi used to sign his poems. Kan (館) means “building, house”. In 1939, his students built a dojo in Tokyo and named it Shōtōkan, “Shōtō’s house”. The dojo was destroyed during the war, in 1945, but the name stayed: today it names the style. His son Gigō (Yoshitaka) brought lower stances and new kicks to it.

After Funakoshi

Funakoshi died in 1957, in Tokyo. His students founded associations that spread Shotokan around the world. Over time, some schools turned toward sport sparring and competition; others stayed true to his focus on kata and self-mastery. The techniques belong to the same family, but the details change from one school to another: when in doubt, your sensei’s instruction is what counts.

Why train?

The main purpose of Shotokan training is self-improvement. It is not first a street self-defence system, nor an Olympic sport: it is long-term work on yourself. Set your own goals and use them to measure your progress. For example:

  • faster reaction time;
  • more flexibility and range of motion;
  • toned muscles and strength that is useful every day;
  • control of your body and your mind;
  • mushin (無心, “no-mind”): being fully absorbed in the movement, with no stray thought;
  • humility, and respect for life and for other martial artists;
  • being able to defend yourself, and your family, without weapons.
