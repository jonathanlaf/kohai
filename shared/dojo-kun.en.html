Dojo Kun 道場訓

The dojo kun (道場訓, “the precepts of the dojo”) fits in five sentences. They are credited to Funakoshi Gichin, the founder of Shotokan karate; their exact origin is debated, and the wording varies a little from one school to another. They often hang on the dojo wall and are recited at the end of class.

They remind us that karate is first an education of character: the technique serves the person, not the other way around. Each precept starts with “Hitotsu” and ends with “koto” (こと, “what one must do”): it is a rule of conduct, not just an idea.

1. Jinkaku kansei 人格完成

一、人格完成に努むること

Hitotsu, jinkaku kansei ni tsutomuru koto

“hee-toh-tsoo, jin-kah-koo kahn-say nee tsoo-toh-moo-roo koh-toh”

Seek perfection of character.

JapaneseKanjiMeaning
Jinkaku人格Character, personality
Kansei完成Completion, perfection
Tsutomuru努むるTo strive for, to work toward
KotoことWhat one must do

The first precept gives the goal of everything else. Nobody ever becomes “perfect”: what counts is the effort toward it, in every class as in everyday life. Some schools write 努めること (tsutomeru): the meaning is the same.

2. Makoto no michi 誠の道

一、誠の道を守ること

Hitotsu, makoto no michi wo mamoru koto

“hee-toh-tsoo, mah-koh-toh noh mee-chee oh mah-moh-roo koh-toh”

Stay faithful to the way of sincerity.

JapaneseKanjiMeaning
Makoto誠Sincerity, truth, loyalty
Michi道Way, path (the same kanji as dō in karate-dō)
Mamoru守るTo protect, to keep, to stay faithful to

This is the hardest precept to translate: makoto brings together honesty, loyalty and genuineness. Be true to others, and to yourself: don’t cheat on a stance, admit what you don’t know yet. The “wo” (を) is pronounced “oh”.

3. Doryoku no seishin 努力の精神

一、努力の精神を養うこと

Hitotsu, doryoku no seishin wo yashinau koto

“hee-toh-tsoo, doh-ryoh-koo noh say-sheen oh yah-shee-now koh-toh”

Cultivate a spirit of effort.

JapaneseKanjiMeaning
Doryoku努力Effort
Seishin精神Spirit, state of mind
Yashinau養うTo cultivate, to nourish, to raise

Yashinau is the word for feeding a child or growing a plant: a spirit of effort can’t be ordered, it grows with steady practice. Repeating the same kata a hundred times, coming back to class after a bad day.

4. Reigi 礼儀

一、礼儀を重んずること

Hitotsu, reigi wo omonzuru koto

“hee-toh-tsoo, ray-ghee oh oh-mohn-zoo-roo koh-toh”

Respect the rules of courtesy.

JapaneseKanjiMeaning
Reigi礼儀Courtesy, etiquette, good manners
Omonzuru重んずるTo value, to hold in esteem

Rei (礼), the bow, opens and closes every kata and every class. Courtesy goes beyond the gesture: respect for the sensei, the senpai, the younger partners and the dojo itself. Karate begins and ends with the bow.

5. Kekki no yū 血気の勇

一、血気の勇を戒むること

Hitotsu, kekki no yū wo imashimuru koto

“hee-toh-tsoo, keck-kee noh yoo oh ee-mah-shee-moo-roo koh-toh”

Guard against violence and reckless courage.

JapaneseKanjiMeaning
Kekki血気“Hot blood”, rashness, impetuousness
Yū勇Courage, bravery
Imashimuru戒むるTo restrain, to guard against

Courage that comes from anger is not courage. A karateka learns to fight so as not to have to: they stay calm, avoid conflict and use their technique only as a last resort. That is the sense of Funakoshi’s maxim “Karate ni sente nashi” (空手に先手なし, “in karate, there is no first attack”).

Why “Hitotsu”? 一つ

Hitotsu means “one”, “first”. The five precepts are not numbered one, two, three: each one starts with “one”. None comes before the others; each is as important as the first. The numbers on this page are only there to find your way.

The recitation

In many dojos, the dojo kun is recited at the end of class, kneeling in seiza, after mokuso (meditation) and before the final bow. One student, often the highest-ranked senpai, calls out each precept; everyone repeats it together, in a strong voice. It is not recited for show: it is a reminder of why we train.

The custom changes from one dojo to another (who recites, when, in Japanese or translated): follow your dojo’s way and your sensei’s instructions.
