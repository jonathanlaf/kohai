The dogi 道着

The dogi (“clothes of the way”) is the karate training uniform. It is also called karategi 空手着 (“karate clothes”) or keikogi 稽古着 (“practice clothes”). You often hear just “gi,” but Gi 着 on its own simply means “clothes”: say dogi or karategi. It is simple, sturdy and the same for everyone: only the belt shows rank.

Parts of the dogi

  • Uwagi 上着: the jacket, crossed over the chest, often closed with strings on the inside.
  • Zubon ズボン: the pants, held at the waist by a drawstring or elastic.
  • Obi 帯: the belt, which keeps the jacket closed and shows rank (see Tying your belt).

Wearing it properly

  • White and plain. In Shotokan, the dogi is white, with no pattern. Patches (dojo, federation) follow the dojo’s rules.
  • Left over right. The left side of the jacket always crosses over the right side. In Japan, the opposite is reserved for dressing the dead.
  • Clean and in good shape, ironed if possible, with no holes or open seams.
  • Nothing underneath for men; women may wear a plain white T-shirt or sports top.
  • Nothing else on you: no jewellery, no watch, no visible piercings. Fingernails and toenails are short, and long hair is tied back.
  • Barefoot on the mat; sandals (zori) stay at the edge.

The right length

The competition rules of the World Karate Federation (WKF), the international body for sport karate, give good guidelines, even for training and even if your association follows other rules:

  • Jacket: with the belt tied, it covers at least the hips, but no more than three-quarters of the thigh.
  • Sleeves: no longer than the bend of the wrist, no shorter than halfway down the forearm. They are not rolled up.
  • Pants: they cover at least two-thirds of the shin, but do not go below the ankle bone. They are not rolled up either.

Choosing a dogi

From a distance every dogi looks the same, but they differ in fabric weight, cut and intended use. Weight is often given in ounces (oz): the higher the number, the thicker the fabric. When you start, a student dogi is plenty; ask your sensei before buying something more expensive.

Japanese or European cut

  • Japanese cut: longer jacket, shorter sleeves and pants. It is the traditional cut, common for kata dogi.
  • European cut: longer sleeves and pants, shorter jacket.

In competition

Every organization has its own uniform rules: follow your association’s, and ask your sensei when in doubt.

  • Traditional Shotokan associations (JKA, JKS, SKIF…): they run their own tournaments. They usually ask for a white, plain, clean dogi, with the patch the association allows.
  • Sport karate (the WKF and its member federations, such as Karate Canada): the dogi must be white, plain and WKF-approved (a label confirms it).

In kumite, one competitor wears a red belt or ribbon (Aka 赤), the other white (Shiro 白) in traditional associations or blue (Ao 青) in sport karate: the dogi is not what tells them apart.

Finding your size

  • Sizes are often given as height in centimetres (160, 170, 180…) or as numbers (roughly 0 to 7). Go by the brand’s size chart.
  • A 100% cotton dogi shrinks in the first wash, often by several percent: buy it a little long, or one size up if you are between two.
  • A cotton and polyester dogi shrinks very little: buy your size.

Care and folding

A clean dogi is a sign of respect for your partners and for the dojo. Well cared for, it lasts for years.

Washing

  • Wash it after every class, inside out, in cold or lukewarm water (30 to 40 °C).
  • Do not leave it damp in your bag: take it out as soon as you get home.
  • Avoid chlorine bleach, which yellows cotton and damages embroidery. An oxygen-based stain remover keeps it bright white.
  • Wash it on its own or with whites, especially the first time.

Drying

  • Avoid the dryer: heat shrinks cotton and wears out the fabric.
  • Hang it on a hanger or a line, out of direct sun if it has embroidery.
  • Iron it while still slightly damp for a crisp dogi.

Folding

The traditional way to fold the dogi keeps it neat and turns it into a small bundle that is easy to carry, held by the belt:

  1. Lay the jacket flat, back down, with the front closed.
  2. Fold the pants in half lengthwise, then lay them in the middle of the jacket, waist towards the collar.
  3. Fold the sleeves over the pants, then fold both sides of the jacket towards the middle.
  4. Fold the whole thing in half or in thirds, from the bottom up to the collar, to make a bundle.
  5. Wrap the belt around the bundle and tie it as you would at the waist.

What about the belt?

A widespread tradition says you do not wash your belt: it keeps the mark of the work you have done. It is a custom, not a rule, and every dojo has its own habits. If you do wash it, do it by hand, in cold water, and let it dry flat: a coloured belt can bleed.

Obi Musubi 帯結び: tying your belt

The belt (Obi 帯) keeps the dogi jacket closed and shows your rank. It is tied with a square knot, also called a reef knot (Hon Musubi 本結び): a knot that does not slip during training and comes undone easily afterwards. This is the most common method; if your sensei teaches another one, follow theirs.

Before you start

  • Close the jacket first: left side over right side, and tie the inside strings if it has any.
  • In the dojo, you adjust your uniform and belt facing the back wall, with your back to the Shomen 正面, never facing the sensei.
  • Fold the belt in half to find its middle.

The steps

  1. Middle on the belly. Place the middle of the belt on your belly, just below the navel, with both ends the same length on each side.
  2. Two turns. Bring both ends behind your back, cross them and bring them back to the front. The belt now goes around your waist twice. Keep the layers flat, one on top of the other, with no twist.
  3. Even out. In front, pull the ends so they are the same length again. Tighten without cutting off your breath: the belt sits at the hips, not at the waist.
  4. Tuck under both turns. Cross the right end over the left end, then slide it from the bottom up under both layers of the belt. Pull: one end now comes out at the top, the other hangs at the bottom.
  5. Make the knot. Fold the top end down, then bring the bottom end over it and up through the loop it makes.
  6. Tighten. Pull both ends out to the sides at the same time to tighten the knot flat.

Check the knot

  • The knot is centred, flat, and does not make a loop.
  • Both ends hang the same length, about a hand (15 to 20 cm), and point out to each side in a nearly horizontal line.
  • If one end points up and the other down, it is a granny knot: undo the last step and cross the other way.
  • At the back, both turns lie flat on top of each other, with no twist.

The belt in the dojo

If your belt comes undone during class, step out of the line, turn towards the back of the dojo and retie it before you continue. After training, fold it rather than throwing it into the bottom of your bag, and keep it off the floor.
