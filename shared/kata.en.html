Katas 型

What is a kata 型?

A kata (型, “form”) is a set sequence of techniques, performed alone against imaginary opponents. It is the memory of karate: each kata passes techniques, stances, movements and fighting principles from one generation of practitioners to the next.

Each kata follows a precise pattern on the floor, the embusen, starts and ends at the same spot, and opens and closes with a bow.

The spirit of the kata

A kata is a fight, not a choreography. Every block stops a real attack, every punch targets a specific opponent. So you perform it with strength, determination and intensity: eyes fixed on the opponent, decisive techniques, the whole body set on the fight. Someone watching your kata should see that fight.

Three Japanese ideas describe this spirit, and together with the accuracy of the stances, they are what a kata is judged on:

  • Kime 決め: the explosive focus of the whole body’s power at the exact moment the technique reaches its target.
  • Kihaku 気迫: the fighting spirit, the intensity and determination that come through the whole kata.
  • Zanshin 残心: the awareness that remains after each technique and until the end of the kata, as if the opponent could still attack.

Announcing the kata

At the start, the sensei announces the kata you are about to perform. Answer by naming it, for example “Heian Shodan!”, in a strong, confident voice. This answer marks the start of the fight and shows your confidence and determination.

The 26 katas

Shotokan has 26 katas, grouped into four families. They are learned in order, as you move up through the grades (see Belts and ranks). The first one is Heian Shodan, at white belt. Within a series, Shodan, Nidan, Sandan, Yondan and Godan number the katas from 1 to 5 (see Shodan and the dan ranks).

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

These mistakes show up in every kata. The list grows as classes go on and I find new ones.

Rushing the count

Each technique must be a clean move, fully finished before the next one starts. Settle each stance: speed comes from crispness, not from hurrying.

A Zenkutsu Dachi 前屈立ち that is too short

Zenkutsu Dachi must be long and low: front knee over the toes, back leg straight, hips facing forward (Shomen). Too short or too high, and you lose all the power the kata is meant to develop.

Striking before the foot is planted

The fist and the hip must arrive together (Kime). A punch thrown too early has no body weight behind it.

Forgetting Hikite 引き手

The hand that is not working snaps back to the hip. That pull is what gives the blocking or striking arm its strength: without it, the technique loses half its power.

A weak or missing Kiai 気合

Each kata’s Kiai (shown in its tutorial) are moments of total commitment, not a polite shout. Examiners listen for them.
